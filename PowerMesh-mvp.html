PowerMesh — Business Model
& MVP Specification

A coordination marketplace for temporary energy capacity during planned and unplanned load shedding. This document defines the business model, MVP scope, API surface, and tech stack for an enterprise-grade, scalable backend assignment.

📍 Target market: Bangladesh (industrial zones) 🏗 Scope: MVP v1.1 — assignment-ready 📅 Deadline: September 07, 2026
Section 01

What PowerMesh is — and what it is not

PowerMesh is a marketplace and orchestration platform for temporary energy capacity during planned or expected electricity shortages. It connects energy providers that have available backup or distributed energy capacity with consumers that need temporary electricity continuity.

PowerMesh manages the commercial and operational workflow end to end: supply discovery → outage event context → capacity offer matching → allocation → reservation → payment → delivery → settlement → failure handling.

PowerMesh does not generate, own, transmit, or physically control electricity. It is the coordination, reservation, payment, and tracking layer. Physical delivery is the provider's responsibility. PowerMesh records whether delivery occurred and handles the financial consequences accordingly.
Regulatory framing Commercial transactions involving electricity supply in Bangladesh are regulated by BERC (Bangladesh Energy Regulatory Commission). PowerMesh must be positioned consistently as a coordination and payment layer — not an electricity reseller. Engage a local energy lawyer before launch.
AI integration path The MVP data model is designed to support future AI features: outage prediction (ML on BPDB/REB historical schedules), dynamic pricing, provider reliability scoring, and anomaly detection on delivery reports. The schema captures the signals now so the models can be trained later.
Section 02

Primary roles

PowerMesh has exactly three primary roles. Role permissions are enforced at the middleware layer — no role can access or mutate resources belonging to another role.

⚡
Provider
Owns or operates an energy resource with genuinely spare capacity during load shedding windows. Registers resources, submits capacity offers, accepts reservations, and confirms or reports delivery.
Resource types supported
Generator, battery storage, solar + BESS, microgrid, or any other backup/distributed energy resource. The model is resource-type agnostic.
🏢
Consumer
An organization that needs temporary electricity continuity during a shedding window. Browses open events in their service area, submits capacity requests, makes payments, and confirms delivery.
Consumer categories
Hospitals, diagnostic centers, telecom facilities, factories, data centers, commercial buildings, apartment complexes, cold chain operators. Criticality determines allocation priority, not consumer type.
🛠
Operator
PowerMesh staff managing the marketplace. Verifies providers and resources, creates and manages outage events, reviews and approves allocations, handles failures, manages disputes, and monitors the platform.
MVP scope
Operator actions are predominantly manual in MVP. The platform surfaces calculated results; the operator reviews and approves before anything becomes binding.
Section 03

Physical compatibility — a hard requirement

A provider cannot serve an arbitrary consumer simply because they exist on the same platform. Electricity requires a physical connection. Provider resources and consumer facilities must belong to a physically serviceable relationship before any matching, offer surfacing, or allocation can occur.

What defines physical compatibility

Physical compatibility means the provider's resource and the consumer's facility share one of the following:

Same service area — operator-defined geographic zone (e.g., Gazipur Industrial Zone, Savar EPZ, DEPZ)
Same industrial estate or campus — both facilities on the same bounded site with shared internal distribution
Same microgrid — both connected to the same islanded or community microgrid segment
Same contiguous distribution segment — on the same feeder or substation zone as confirmed during provider verification
Explicit direct connection — provider and consumer have a documented, verified direct physical interconnect

How compatibility is enforced in the system

At registration, the Operator assigns each provider resource and each consumer facility to a serviceZoneId. This zone tag is the compatibility key. The matching engine will not surface a provider's offer to a consumer unless both share the same serviceZoneId. This is a hard filter, not a soft preference — it cannot be overridden by the allocation algorithm or by either party.

Zone definition is operator responsibility The accuracy of the service zone assignment determines whether the platform's reservations are physically deliverable. Operators must verify zone assignments during resource onboarding — not after the first delivery failure.
Section 04

Capacity offers — the fundamental supply unit

Providers do not create "load-shedding slots." They create time-bound capacity offers. An offer represents a provider's willingness to supply a defined amount of capacity, from a specific resource, at a specific price, within a defined time window.

The outage event creates the marketplace context — a named shortage in a service area and time window. The outage event does not create supply. Supply comes entirely from provider capacity offers that are matched against the event.

Offer types

Type Definition Example
Standing / recurring offer A recurring availability schedule that activates automatically when a matching outage event is created. Remains active until the provider withdraws or modifies it. "200 kW, every weekday 18:00–22:00, ৳X per kW/hour" — active for any qualifying event in this window.
Event-specific offer Capacity offered in response to a specific outage event. Created after an event is published. Does not recur. "150 kW for Outage Event #47, September 10, 19:00–21:00, ৳Y per kW/hour."

Offer fields

Every capacity offer — regardless of type — carries: the provider resource ID, the service zone ID, available capacity in kW, price per kW/hour, and the valid time window. The system checks for time conflicts against the same resource's existing offers before accepting a new one.

Outage event vs. supply — why they are separate Keeping them separate means the marketplace does not start from zero each time an event is created. Standing offers from verified providers are already in the system. When an operator creates or confirms an event, relevant offers surface immediately without requiring manual provider action every time.
Section 05

Outage events

An outage event represents a planned or expected electricity shortage in a specific service area and time window. It is the marketplace context — not the supply. The operator creates or confirms events. Providers do not create events.

Event creation sources

Source MVP Future
Operator manually creates event Yes Yes
Operator imports from BPDB/REB schedule Yes — manual CSV Automated API polling
Provider self-reports outage start Yes — flags for operator review Yes
Smart meter / grid telemetry No Phase 2+
ML/AI outage prediction No Phase 3+ (data exists, model is future)

When no outage occurs

If the main grid stays on during a confirmed shedding window, and the provider confirms no delivery was needed, the consumer is refunded in full. Platform fee is not charged. This must be stated explicitly in the consumer terms of service before launch.

Section 06

MVP workflow

Eight steps, two parties, one operator. Every step is logged. Every state transition is auditable.

1
Provider onboarding and resource verification
Provider registers and submits their energy resource: type (generator, battery, solar+BESS, microgrid, etc.), physical location, rated capacity in kW, and supporting documentation. Operator verifies the resource, assigns it to a service zone (serviceZoneId), and marks it as verified. An unverified resource cannot create capacity offers.
Provider Operator verification
2
Provider creates capacity offer
Verified provider creates a standing offer (recurring schedule) or an event-specific offer: available kW, price per kW/hour, and time window. The system checks for time conflicts on the same resource. The offer is active until withdrawn, expired, or fully allocated. Offers are tagged with the resource's serviceZoneId.
Provider
3
Operator creates or confirms outage event
Operator creates an event for a specific service zone and time window. On event creation, standing offers from verified providers in the same zone whose time windows overlap are automatically surfaced and linked to the event. The marketplace is now open — consumers in the same zone can submit requests.
Operator
4
Consumer submits capacity request
Consumer selects an open outage event in their service zone, specifies required capacity in kW and required time window. The system validates that the consumer's registered facility belongs to the event's service zone — if not, the request is rejected. Requests are held as pending until allocation runs.
Consumer
5
Allocation runs — system calculates, operator approves
When the allocation window closes (configurable, e.g. 2 hours before the outage), the system runs the allocation algorithm: filter by physical compatibility (same service zone), match time windows, rank consumers by criticality tier, apply first-come-first-served within tiers. The algorithm outputs an allocation plan. The Operator reviews and approves it before it becomes binding — no allocation is confirmed without operator sign-off in MVP.
System calculates Operator approves
6
Reservation confirmed — consumer pays via payment gateway
Allocated consumers receive a confirmed reservation and a payment window (e.g. 60 minutes). Consumer initiates payment via the standard gateway flow (bKash / SSLCommerz / Stripe). The system uses idempotency keys to prevent double-charges on retries. On payment success callback, the reservation moves to Confirmed. On failure or timeout, the reservation is cancelled and the capacity is released to the next eligible consumer.
System Consumer pays Provider notified
7
Delivery window — manual check-in by both parties
The outage window begins. The provider activates their resource and checks in via the app to confirm delivery has started. Consumer receives a notification. At the end of the window, the provider submits a delivery report: actual kW delivered, start time, end time. No IoT, no smart metering, no automated grid monitoring in MVP.
Provider Consumer notified
8
Consumer confirms or disputes — settlement runs
Consumer reviews the provider's delivery report and confirms or disputes within a defined window (e.g. 24 hours). On confirmation: payment is released to the provider minus the platform fee. On dispute: operator reviews and decides. Partial delivery triggers proportional settlement — consumer refunded the difference, provider paid for actual delivery only. All outcomes are recorded to the provider's reliability history.
Provider Consumer Operator (if disputed)
Section 07

Allocation rules

Allocation is deterministic and documented. When supply is insufficient to satisfy all demand, the algorithm applies these criteria in order: (1) physical compatibility, (2) time window compatibility, (3) consumer criticality tier, (4) first-come-first-served within the same tier. The result is a calculated plan that the operator reviews before it becomes binding.

Allocation does not use a highest-bidder mechanism. Price is set by the provider at offer creation — allocation is not a price auction.

Consumer criticality tier
Examples

Consumer criticality tier is assigned by the operator during consumer onboarding. A consumer cannot self-assign their own tier.

Partial allocation If a consumer requests 200 kW but only 150 kW is available at their priority level, the system can offer a partial allocation. The consumer must explicitly accept or reject the partial offer — silence is rejection and the capacity is released. Partial acceptance is recorded and used in settlement.
Section 08

Reservation and payment lifecycle

Every state transition is logged with a timestamp, actor, and reason. This log is the audit trail for disputes, refunds, and provider reliability tracking.

Normal flow

Pending allocation → Allocated → Payment pending → Confirmed → Active → Completed

Payment failure or timeout

Payment pending → Cancelled — capacity released

Released capacity is offered to the next eligible consumer in the priority queue. Idempotency keys prevent double-charge on retry.

Provider failure (first-class workflow)

Active → Provider failed → Consumer refunded in full

Provider failure is recorded as an incident. The refund is processed automatically via the payment gateway. The incident is added to the provider's reliability history. Three confirmed failures in 90 days triggers operator review for suspension. The consumer does not bear the financial loss of a provider failure under any circumstances.

Partial delivery

Active → Partially delivered → Settled — proportional

Consumer is refunded the proportion of payment corresponding to undelivered capacity. Provider receives payment for actual delivery only. The shortfall is recorded as a partial delivery incident.

Consumer cancellation

Confirmed → Cancelled by consumer

More than 4 hours before window: full refund. Within 4 hours: 50% platform fee retained. Policy must be stated in consumer terms of service before launch.

Outage did not occur

Confirmed → Voided — full refund

If the main grid stayed on and the provider confirms no delivery was needed, the consumer is refunded in full. No platform fee is charged.

Section 11

Tech stack — assignment-aligned

Runtime
Node.js + TypeScript
Type safety on the allocation logic, reservation lifecycle, and payment flows prevents entire classes of runtime bugs.
Framework
Express.js
Modular architecture: routes → controllers → services → Prisma. No magic, easy to reason about, easy to test.
Database
PostgreSQL + Prisma ORM
Relational model maps cleanly to the allocation logic. Prisma transactions prevent race conditions on concurrent reservation creation. Indexed on serviceZoneId, eventId, status.
Validation
Zod
Schema-first validation with TypeScript inference. Consistent structured error messages on all endpoints.
Authentication
JWT (access + refresh) + GCP OAuth2
Three-role system enforced via middleware. Refresh token rotation. GCP social login for consumer onboarding.
Payments
SSLCommerz (primary) — bKash as secondary
SSLCommerz has the most complete sandbox environment for assignment testing. Idempotency keys on all payment webhook handlers prevent duplicate charge processing.
Caching
Redis
Cache service zone + event listings (high read, low write). Rate limiting via express-rate-limit + Redis store. Session/token blacklist on logout.
Security
Helmet + CORS + express-rate-limit
Security headers, origin whitelist, per-IP rate limiting on auth endpoints. Passwords hashed with bcrypt. Secrets in environment variables only.
File storage
Multer + Cloudinary
Provider resource documentation uploads (ownership proof, site photos). Stored in Cloudinary, URL saved in DB.
Email
Nodemailer / Resend
Transactional emails: registration confirmation, reservation confirmation, refund notification, delivery report.
Linting
Biome
Fast, zero-config linting + formatting. One tool instead of ESLint + Prettier.
Documentation
Postman collection
All 28 endpoints documented with example requests, responses, and environment variables for all three roles.
Deployment
Render (backend) + Neon / Supabase (PostgreSQL)
Render supports persistent Node.js servers (not just serverless) — important for WebSocket or long-running processes in future. Neon provides serverless PostgreSQL with free tier.
Section 12

What MVP does not do

Feature MVP Rationale
Pre-funded wallet / stored balance Out Standard gateway flow with idempotency is simpler, safer, and sufficient. Wallet can be added in Phase 1.
Real-time delivery monitoring / IoT Out Manual check-in is sufficient and avoids hardware dependency.
Automated allocation (no operator review) Out Operator-reviewed allocation builds trust before removing the human from the loop.
Provider reliability score algorithm Out Raw delivery counts are recorded. Scoring requires sufficient data to be meaningful.
AI / ML outage forecasting Out Data model captures the signals. Model comes in Phase 3 when training data exists.
Dynamic pricing Out Provider sets fixed price per offer. Dynamic pricing is a Phase 2 feature.
Multiple service zones Soft limit The schema supports multiple zones. Start with one to validate the loop.
Physical grid switching / control Never Outside PowerMesh's scope permanently. PowerMesh is the coordination and payment layer.
Manual delivery confirmation (provider + consumer) In Simple, auditable, requires no hardware.
Provider failure + proportional refund flow In First-class workflow. Consumer trust depends on this from day one.
Operator dispute resolution In Required from launch. Cannot operate without a dispute path.
Audit log on all critical state changes In Mandatory for enterprise-grade accountability and assignment requirements.
Soft deletes on all records In No hard deletes. deletedAt timestamp on all entities. Required by assignment.

Key risks and mitigations

Severity
Mitigation
No genuine spare capacity on the supply side
High
Validate with 5–10 provider conversations before building. If surplus doesn't exist at meaningful scale, the model needs rethinking first.

Section 15

PowerMesh in one paragraph

PowerMesh is a marketplace where verified energy providers — operators of generators, batteries, solar+BESS systems, microgrids, or any backup energy resource — create time-bound capacity offers, and organizations that need temporary electricity continuity during load shedding request that capacity. Physical compatibility is a hard requirement: providers and consumers must share the same operator-verified service zone. Outage events create the marketplace context; they do not create supply — supply comes from standing and event-specific provider offers. Allocation is deterministic, criticality-based, and operator-reviewed. Consumers pay via a standard payment gateway with idempotency. Delivery is confirmed manually by both parties. Provider failure triggers a full consumer refund and a recorded incident. The MVP is a 28-endpoint REST API built on Node.js, TypeScript, Express, PostgreSQL, Prisma, and Redis — enterprise-grade from the first commit, and designed to support AI-powered features as the data accumulates.
